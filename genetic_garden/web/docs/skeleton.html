The skeleton

Branch geometry and organ placement: three growth rules, a walk with forces on it, and a draw order that does more work than it looks like it should.

source: genetic_garden/skeleton.py · every figure on this page is drawn by the code it describes

1Walking a branch

Every branch is traced forward one step at a time. At each step three forces turn the heading, and the sum of them over eight steps is the difference between a branch and a line segment.

The constant per-branch curvature is the branch's own handwriting — it is what makes two limbs of the same plant look related. Phototropism and gravity are the world acting on it, and gravity is weighted by slenderness, so a trunk resists what a twig cannot.

2From a spine to a shape

A traced spine plus a width at each vertex becomes a closed outline: offset along the normals, up one flank and back down the other.

The end treatment is not cosmetic. A branch with something continuing from its tip is left blunt, so the next segment's collar can cover the cut; only a genuine terminal twig tapers to a point.

3Three rules, three kingdoms

There is no shared branching function with a kingdom flag. Each kingdom has its own rule, because they differ in what they do rather than in their parameters.

  • Tree — monopodial. One dominant trunk, laterals along its upper part, and a leader that carries the axis onward. First-order limbs are deliberately held to 70% of their natural length, or the tree reads as a bush with a stick under it.
  • Bush — sympodial, and no trunk at all. Several stems straight from the ground, each forking at the tip with nothing leading. The basal fan is most of what separates a bush silhouette from a small tree.
  • Flower — a single slender stem, a basal rosette, few but large leaves, and one terminal bloom that is the subject of the drawing.

4Randomness with an address

Branches do not draw from a shared random stream. Each derives its own from where it sits in the tree.

With a shared stream, growing a plant one level deeper appends draws in the middle of a traversal and reshuffles every branch after that point. The plant would stop being the same individual from one contribution to the next — which is the one thing growth must never do.

5The junction problem

Getting branches to look grown rather than assembled took three separate things, and the first attempt fixed the wrong one.

The obvious culprit is the branch base: a flat cut, perpendicular to the branch, sitting on the trunk. But zooming into a real render showed the actual seam was somewhere else entirely — the trunk's own tip, pinched to 35% width where its leader took over.

6Draw order is the algorithm

the ordering

Limbs passing behind a branch are emitted before it. Its own continuation is emitted after it.

That gives: back limbs deepest-first, then the branch over their bases, then its leader over its cut tip, then the limbs that cross in front. A lateral emerges from behind its parent; a leader covers what its parent left open.

So skeleton.branches is a draw list, not a bag, and the renderer iterates it as-is. There is a test asserting this, because sorting the list is an easy and invisible way to put every seam back.

for spec in specs:
    if not spec['front']:
        _tree_branch(...)      # limbs passing behind
sk.branches.append(me)         # this branch
_tree_branch(**leader)         # its continuation
for spec in specs:
    if spec['front']:
        _tree_branch(...)      # limbs crossing in front

7Collars and buttresses

On top of the ordering, a limb flares where it leaves its parent and starts from a point behind its origin, inside the parent's wood. That is the botanical branch collar, and it is what stops the graft look.

Two details had to be tuned after the first pass. A limb crossing in front of its parent has nothing hiding its buried end, so a wide wedge just drew a seam from the other direction — front limbs get a thin spike instead. And giving leaders a full collar made every trunk joint bulge like a knuckle, so a leader flares 0.12 against a lateral's 0.40.

8Foliage, and what is not drawn

Crowns are carried by soft masses fitted to clusters of branch tips, not by drawing every leaf. Tips are snapped to a grid so a dense twiggy region yields one fat blob rather than fifty overlapping ones, and the drawn leaves sit on top and break the edge.

The foliage budget is counted in blades rather than leaves, so a compound-leaved tree gets fewer, larger fronds instead of costing five times as much as a simple one. Leaves are then culled to that budget, keeping the outermost preferentially — but only just over half of them, because a crown whose leaves all sit on the perimeter looks like sprigs stuck round a blob.
